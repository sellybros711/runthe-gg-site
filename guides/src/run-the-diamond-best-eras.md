Decades Draft lets you pick one decade and draft only from it. So which decade gives you the most for your money? The short answer: no decade is cheaper. What changes is what the wheel offers you. This guide shows how prices work, where each era is thin, and how to read an era card before you pick.

## A price doesn't care what year it is

Every player's price comes from one formula: 1.5 times his WAR to the power 1.6. Nobody costs less than $1M. The year never goes in. A 4 WAR shortstop costs the same in 1925 as in 2015.

The curve bends upward, so stars cost more per win:

- **2 WAR:** about $4.5M
- **4 WAR:** about $13.8M
- **6 WAR:** about $26.4M
- **10 WAR:** about $59.7M

You have $190M for twelve spots. That's about $15.8M a spot, which buys a man near 4.4 WAR. The most expensive man in the game is Babe Ruth's 1923 at $103.7M. He alone eats more than half the cap.

So "era X is cheaper per WAR" is never true. At equal WAR, every decade charges the same.

## Read "counts as", not the big number

There's one catch that makes old pitchers look cheap. A starter who threw more than 210 innings is priced and rated on what he did in 210. His tile still shows his whole season. Under it, in green, is what he counts as here.

Take Ed Walsh's 1908. The tile says 10.3 WAR. He counts as 4.7, and he costs $17.6M. That's the price of a 4.7 WAR man, because that's what he is in this game. Walter Johnson's 1913 shows 15.2 and counts as 9.2.

This hits the early decades hardest. On boards the wheel can draw, 59% of 1900s starters threw over 210 innings. In the 2010s it's 9%. An old ace isn't a bargain. Judge every arm by the green number.

## What a decade really changes

Since price is fixed, an era's value is what lands on your boards. Every spin draws one real team-season from your decade, and you take one man from it. Three things move from decade to decade.

**Depth.** The card shows how many team seasons the wheel can land on. Six seasons of the 2020s give it 175. The 2010s give it 300. A thin decade repeats clubs more and offers fewer big seasons.

**Shape.** The card draws its top 40 seasons as bats against arms. A lopsided decade wears a chip: Pitchers' decade or Hitters' decade. In a pitchers' decade aces are easy to find. Good bats are rarer.

**Holes.** Some slots barely exist in some decades. The closer is the big one.

## The dead-ball closer hole

Your closer must be a reliever. In the 1900s and 1910s, teams hardly used them. The best closer on an average board from those decades is worth less than 0.1 WAR. Fewer than one board in a hundred has a 3 WAR closer.

So don't hunt for one. Take the cheapest reliever who fits and spend the money on starters instead. Those decades are full of them: 84% of 1900s boards have a starter worth 3 WAR or more.

A closer still matters elsewhere. His save rate starts at 80% and gains 2 points for each WAR, up to 95%. From the 1960s to the 2000s, a 3 WAR closer turns up on about one board in five.

## The thin spots, decade by decade

These come from every team-season the wheel can draw, counting each pitcher by his green number.

- **1920s:** the weakest decade for starters. A board's best starter averages 3.3 WAR, the lowest of any decade.
- **1940s:** catchers are scarce. Only one board in ten has a 3 WAR catcher.
- **1970s:** the best decade for catchers. About a third of boards have a 3 WAR one.
- **2020s:** the thinnest decade overall. A board's best man averages 5.1 WAR, against 5.6 to 6.1 everywhere else. Only 34% of boards hold a 6 WAR season. Starters are scarce as well.

## Chemistry inside a decade

Chemistry works a little differently here. Two links stop counting in Decades Draft. Same era is gone, since everyone shares one. Played together (team-mates from different seasons) is off too.

What's left is what you choose:

- **Family:** +9
- **Reunion (same club, same season):** +8
- **Batterymates:** +7
- **Double-play combo:** +6
- **College:** +4
- **Same franchise:** +3

The team total tops out at +12. A decade with fewer clubs repeats shirts more often. The 1900s and 1950s have 16 clubs, and the 1990s on have 30. So a reunion, a battery or a franchise tie comes easier in an old, small league.

## How to judge a decade

- **Ignore the price per WAR.** It's the same everywhere.
- **Check the team seasons on the card.** More seasons means more choice on every spin.
- **Read the bats and arms bar.** Plan to buy the side the decade is short on.
- **Know the hole.** Before the 1920s, punt the closer. In the 2020s, grab a good starter when you see one.
- **Trust the green number.** A heavy starter's big WAR isn't what you get.
- **Spend the cap.** Money left at the end does nothing.
