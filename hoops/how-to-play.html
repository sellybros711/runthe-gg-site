How to play Run The Floor

Spin for a club and a season, sign one player off that roster, and do it six times. Then play all 82 games. The whole game is what happens in between: six great players are not a team, and this one is built to know the difference.

The rules

  1. The wheel gives you a club and a season, anywhere from 1972 to 2023. You sign one player off that roster.
  2. Six spins fill six spots. Five starters and the first man off the bench:
PGSGSF PFC6TH
  1. Every signing comes out of a $145M cap, which is roughly the real one. The most expensive player in the game costs $60M, so one superstar eats 41% of your roster and the other five have to fit under $85M.
  2. You may re-spin three times, for $5M, then $10M, then $15M, charged against the same cap. A re-spin burns the club you turned down.
  3. The sixth man slot takes anybody, and he plays about 72% of a starter's minutes. It is the cheapest place to hide a star and the easiest place to waste one.
  4. No roster may hold more than two of the same position, and no club may give up more than two players across a whole run.
  5. Then you play 82 games against real team-seasons out of the same data. Win 50 and you are a top-six seed. Win 43 and you are in the play-in. Below that is the lottery.

The cap is the game. Taking the best man on every board runs to about $300M and busts before the season starts. You get one great player comfortably, two if you are careful about everything else, and three only by filling the rest of the roster with minimum contracts and hoping.

Six great players are not a team

Most draft games stop at "who is worth the most". This one does not, because that is not how a basketball roster works, and every fan already knows it. Five things get measured after the sixth signature, and every one of them is an argument people have been having about actual NBA teams for forty years.

The ball only bounces once

An NBA team takes about 88 shots a night, and a six man core accounts for roughly 72 of them. Every player carries the shots he actually took. Sign six men who each took twenty a night on their own team and you have a roster asking for 120 shots that do not exist, and the game charges you for every one of them. This is the single largest penalty in the model, and it is the reason a lineup of Jordan, Harden, Bryant, Malone and O'Neal is a bad basketball team rather than the best one ever assembled.

Spacing, measured against the player's own era

Somebody has to be able to shoot or the paint is a car park. But the three-point line did not exist until 1980, and Jerry West attempted zero of them in 1972 because nobody could. That is a fact about the league, not a flaw in Jerry West.

So spacing is relative. A player is compared to what his own league shot, on two counts: how many threes he took against his era's volume, and what share of his own shots came from out there. Steve Kerr took only 2.9 a night, which is unremarkable, but they were half his attempts, which is not. A player from before the line comes back exactly neutral: neither credited nor punished for a shot nobody was taking.

Rim protection is an anchor, not a sum

Five men with 0.4 blocks each do not add up to a rim protector. One man with two of them is one. The game reads the best shot blocker on your roster rather than the total, which is why Ben Wallace or Hakeem Olajuwon changes a defense in a way that five willing helpers do not.

Somebody has to make the pass

Same shape: a primary creator is a person, not a committee. A roster whose best passer averages two assists is five men taking turns. The roster's total assists matter too, but less, because the question a defense asks is who is going to make the read.

The glass

The oldest argument in the sport, and the simplest one here: a core six should account for about 34 rebounds a night. Below that you are giving away possessions.

Era translation

A 1972 team played about 108 possessions a night. A 1999 team played 89. Comparing raw per-game numbers across that gap hands the 1970s an enormous unearned advantage in every counting stat, so every number in this game is pace-adjusted into one common league before anything is measured.

What your roster plays

When the sixth man signs, the game looks at what you have actually built and names it. These are real systems that real teams won with, detected from the players in front of you rather than picked off a menu. Each one is worth a fraction of a rating point, which is a reward for building something coherent and never a substitute for building something good.

Chemistry

Players who share something real fit together faster. Brothers and fathers and sons are worth the most, then actual teammates from the same club in the same season, then a backcourt or a frontcourt that genuinely played together, then a shared college, then the same club in different years, then a draft class.

It saturates, hard. Six players off one roster collect fifteen links and get paid for about three of them, because otherwise stacking one team-season would beat every other decision in the draft. The ceiling is worth about seven wins across a season, which is a real prize and nowhere near enough to replace talent.

The season, and what happens after it

Your 82 games are drawn from real team-seasons in the same dataset, weighted toward the better ones, with eighteen marquee nights against the very best of them. Home and away alternate. A win is worth what a win is worth.

Finish with 50 and you enter the playoffs as a top-six seed: four rounds, all best of seven. Finish with 43 to 49 and you play a single play-in game to get there. The opponent stiffens every round, and stiffens further if your roster is not good enough to belong, so a ring means you built something rather than that you got hot for two months.

Seventy-two wins is the 1996 Bulls. Seventy-three is the 2016 Warriors. Seventy-four has never happened.
