Mockup, nothing wired up

Pixel helmets as profile pictures

One drawing, eighty three paint jobs, and a shelf of unlockables behind it.

For College Football: Perfect Season. Nothing on this page is in the game: it lives in mockups/helmets/ so it can be looked at, argued with and thrown away without touching a thing.

The idea

A helmet is not eighty three drawings. The shell, the ear hole, the crown, the cage and where a mark sits are the same for every school; what changes is four colours, a stripe and a small mark. So the shape is drawn once, by hand, as a grid of characters, and a school is five fields. That is what makes a whole country possible in an afternoon instead of a month, and what makes a throwback just another row of colours.

The angle is turned just far enough to see the crown, which is what puts the stripe on screen: dead side on, a helmet stripe is a line along the top edge and might as well not be there. And the cage sticks out past the front of the shell with nothing behind it, because that is the one silhouette everybody recognises.

Why it looks lit

Flat fill is what a shape looks like with nothing shining on it, and that was the last thing wrong with these. A helmet is close to a sphere, so every pixel gets a normal worked out from where it sits on the dome, dotted against a light up and to the left, and quantised to five tones. Five and not a gradient: the bands are the style. The ramp is anchored so the middle tone is the school's actual colour, which is what keeps a crimson helmet crimson instead of turning it into a pink one with a red edge. Then the edge takes another tone off where the shell turns away, the whole silhouette gets a dark outline so it sits on a background rather than floating in front of one, and four pixels of near white go on by hand at the top left. That last part is the only bit not derived from the geometry, for the same reason a painter puts the catchlight in an eye by hand.

At the sizes it would actually be used

A profile picture is small. The whole thing is judged at the last two, not the first: if it does not survive 30 pixels it does not work, however good the big one looks.

Retro and specials, as unlockables

The same fields, different values. A retro is a helmet a school really wore; a special is one nobody ever did. Locked ones are drawn and drained of colour rather than hidden, because a shelf you can see is a reason to play and an empty one is not.

What is honest about this mockup and what is not.
  • The shape and the lighting are real work and would ship roughly as they are. The facemask took four goes and each wrong one is worth knowing: an even grid of four openings is a window, not a face; a cage with a border round it is a picture frame stuck on a ball; and a cage whose gaps are filled with a dark face colour welds itself into one slab on a dark page. You can see through a facemask. The gaps are nothing at all, so the bars are lines with daylight round them and the mask joins the silhouette instead of decorating it.
  • Thirty two schools are done by hand and the other fifty one are generated from their two colours: primary shell, secondary stripe, initials on the side. Every generated one says generated from its colours underneath, so nothing is claiming to be a real helmet when it is a guess.
  • College suits this better than the pros do. Half these schools really do wear a single letter, and a letter is the one thing a nine pixel box is good at. The same renderer aimed at the NFL game wanted a leaping panther and a raven at that size, which is a different and much harder job.
  • Nothing is unlockable yet. The lock states below are drawn on, not earned. What they would hang off is the badge system the game already has.

If this goes ahead

In rough order: finish the hand list so every school in the game is right, check each against a photograph, draw the handful of marks that are not letters, decide what unlocks what, then a picker. Storage is one short text column on the account, the same shape as the school colours the game already keeps, and the helmet is drawn from it on the fly rather than stored as an image.
